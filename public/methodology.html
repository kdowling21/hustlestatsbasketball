Methodology

How the numbers work

Every metric on Hustle Stats, how it is built, and how much of it is skill versus noise. The full method notes, with live tables, also sit at the bottom of each tool.

Data

Play-by-play comes from NBA.com's live data feed: every shot, foul, turnover, rebound and substitution for the 2020-21 through 2025-26 regular seasons (7,230 games), retrieved through the public nba_data archive. The 2026-27 season is pulled from NBA.com every Monday.

Player tracking (deflections, loose balls, screen assists, contests, box-outs, contested rebounds, distance and speed) comes from NBA.com's tracking stats, pulled month by month so recent games can be weighted more.

Totals are checked against official box scores, and the two sources are checked against each other: charges drawn match player by player (r = 0.995).

Shot Profile

Shot Profile measures points per shot attempt above a baseline and splits the gap into five parts, all in points per 100 attempts. An attempt is a field-goal attempt or a shooting-foul trip.

Decisions

  • Selection: takes shot types worth more than an average attempt.
  • Alignment: takes more of the shots where he beats the baseline and fewer where he doesn't.
  • Foul drawing: gets to the line on shots, valued at baseline FT%.

Execution

  • Making: finishing by zone, on a baseline shot mix.
  • FT making: his FT% against baseline, times his shooting-foul free throws.

Total = Decisions + Execution. The five parts add up exactly to the difference between his points per attempt and the baseline's.

Zones and shot types

Zones use NBA's own tags: restricted area, paint, mid-range, corner 3 and above-the-break 3. Shot-type mode splits those further with the play-by-play descriptors (cuts and lobs, putbacks, drives, floaters, post-ups, catch-and-shoot, pull-up and step-back threes), and grades each shot only against the same shot type.

Adjusted vs raw

By default (Adjusted), each zone's FG% and his FT% are pulled toward what players in a similar role make, by an amount set by how many attempts he has there: a 2-for-3 corner-three sample barely moves him, 300 rim attempts mostly speak for themselves. How hard each zone pulls is estimated from the league data each season. This is why PPA can differ slightly from what a box score gives you.

The Numbers toggle switches to Raw: the same analysis on his actual makes. One change: in Raw, Alignment is folded into Making. With unadjusted makes, a 0-for-3 in a zone a center almost never uses can swing Making and Alignment by 30+ points in opposite directions, so the split stops meaning anything. Totals don't depend on the split. Raw is the better read for high-volume players and for checking a number against the box score; Adjusted is the better read for small samples.

Garbage time

Player numbers drop garbage time: 4th-quarter margins of 25+ in the first 3 minutes, 20+ in the next 3, or 15+ in the last 6. Team numbers keep every possession, the way team ratings are usually reported.

Does it repeat?

Year-to-year correlation of single-season scores (players with 450+ attempts in both seasons), across all five season pairs since 2020-21: Decisions 0.83 to 0.88, Execution 0.65 to 0.72, Total 0.73 to 0.79. Shot choices are one of the most stable things a player does.

Shot difficulty

Shot Profile's last two leaderboard columns, and its Shot-Making and Tough Shots leaderboards, come from NBA.com shot tracking. They sit beside the play-by-play model and never feed into it.

Every field-goal attempt gets an expected value: the league make rate for shots with the same closest-defender distance (0–2, 2–4, 4–6 or 6+ feet), dribbles before the shot (0, 1, 2, 3–6, 7+) and shot type (3-pointer, 2 from 10+ feet, 2 inside 10 feet).

  • Difficulty: league points per shot minus his expected points per shot, per 100 shots. Positive means harder shots.
  • xMaking: his actual points minus expected points, per 100 shots, pulled toward zero by its noise.
  • Tough Shots: the hardest quarter of all shots by expected value, such as tightly guarded pull-up 3s and drives into a defender.

Defender distance matters most at the 4-foot line. With the shot mix held fixed, eFG% runs about 65% wide open, 59% at 4–6 feet, 51% at 2–4 feet and 49% inside 2 feet (2023-24 to 2025-26).

Does it repeat?

Difficulty is one of the most repeatable numbers on the site (year-to-year r = 0.91 to 0.93): a player's shot diet is a fingerprint. Shot-making over expected is real skill but noisier (0.59 to 0.69), so it is shrunk like every other rate here.

Clutch

Shot Profile's Situation toggle switches to Clutch: the same analysis on clutch attempts only, using the NBA's definition (the last 5 minutes of the 4th quarter or overtime, score within 5). Players only, Zones mode only, and the live season is left out until it has enough clutch attempts.

  • Baseline: the league's clutch average, so Decisions compares his clutch shot choices with what a typical clutch attempt is worth.
  • Adjustment: each clutch zone FG% and FT% is pulled toward his own full-game rate (not his role peers), using the same zone weights as the full-game view. A player is assumed to shoot like himself in the clutch unless his clutch sample says otherwise.
  • Minimum: 30 clutch attempts per season to qualify (about 90 players a season). Combined seasons are the best read.

Does it repeat? Clutch Total repeats year to year at r = 0.36 to 0.48 (players qualifying in both seasons), Clutch Decisions at 0.28 to 0.61. Clutch Execution repeats more (0.52 to 0.73), but mostly because it leans on his normal shot-making; the clutch-only part is small and noisy.

Play types

Shot Profile's Play types view uses NBA.com's play-type data (from Synergy): each player's possessions and points in isolation, pick-and-roll (ball handler and roll man), post-ups, spot-ups, transition, cuts, off screens, handoffs, putbacks and other plays. A possession ends in a shot, a shooting foul or a turnover. This data comes as season totals, so it can't be split shot by shot like the zones.

  • Adjusted PPP: his points per possession in a play type, pulled toward the league rate for that play type: (points + k × league PPP) / (possessions + k). k is estimated from the data for each play type (about 70 to 310 possessions), so a 20-possession hot streak barely moves him.
  • Mix: what his play-type diet is worth at league PPP, minus the league average possession, per 100 possessions. Roll men and cutters score high because those plays are efficient for everyone.
  • Efficiency: how far above the league rate he scores within each play type (adjusted), weighted by his mix, per 100 possessions. Mix + Efficiency = Total.
  • Percentiles need 25+ possessions in that play type per season.

Does it repeat? Mix is very stable (year-to-year r = 0.85 to 0.94: it mostly reflects role). Efficiency repeats at 0.66 to 0.69 (players with 500+ possessions in both seasons), about the same as Shot Profile's Execution.

Hustle Plus-Minus

Hustle Plus-Minus (HPM) is the number of points per 100 possessions a player adds through hustle, compared with an average player, split into offense (O-HPM) and defense (D-HPM).

  • O-HPM (offensive hustle): offensive rebounding impact, screen assists and offensive loose balls.
  • D-HPM (defensive hustle): steals, blocks (blocks his team recovers count most), charges and other offensive fouls drawn, defensive rebounding impact, deflections, loose balls and fouls.

Views

The default Current view blends all six seasons, with games 550 days old counting half, so it leans on recent play without throwing away the larger sample. Single seasons are also available.

Fair comparisons

Every hustle stat is pulled toward the average of the player's 25 most similar role peers, in proportion to how little data stands behind it, so a short hot streak can't top the list. Peer pct is the share of those peers whose HPM is below his.

Rebounding impact

oREB and dREB Impact are lineup-adjusted: a ridge regression (RAPM) over about 1.46 million possessions with all ten players on the floor, controlling for score margin and home court, with games 550 days old counting half. One percentage point of team offensive rebound rate is worth 0.58 points per 100 possessions.

Plays without a direct value

Deflections, screen assists, loose balls, contests and box-outs get the weight the data says they earn: how much each predicts lineup-adjusted impact beyond steals, blocks, charges and rebounding. Each weight is pulled toward zero by its uncertainty:

weight used = learned weight × z² / (1 + z²)

Most of these earn very little once steals and rebounding are counted. The deflections that matter become steals, and good box-outs show up as rebounds. They stay on the site as their own leaderboards.

Does it repeat?

Year-to-year correlation of single-season HPM (2,000+ possessions in both seasons): 0.69 to 0.71. Blocks (0.87 to 0.92) and screen assists (0.93 to 0.96) repeat most; rebounding impact (0.39 to 0.54) least.

Matchups (Toughest Defenders)

The Matchups page ranks defenders on three parts. The Toughest Defender Index averages them in standard units (how far each is from average, in standard deviations, among qualified defenders in the view), so the size of a gap counts and not just the order. It is shown as a percentile of that average. Equal thirds are a design choice: the page is about the toughest assignments as well as the best results, and we kept the weights simple and visible rather than tuning them.

1. Assignment difficulty

From NBA.com's matchup tracking, which records who guarded whom on every possession. For each defender: how many points per 100 possessions the players he guarded normally score, compared with a typical assignment for his position. Each scorer's norm is his scoring against everyone else that season, so a defender's own results don't feed his difficulty. Star share is the share of his matchup time on the league's 50 highest-scoring players. Difficulty repeats strongly from season to season (r = 0.72 to 0.77).

2. Matchup results

Points per 100 matchup possessions kept off the board, compared with each scorer's norm and with defenders at the same position: shot-making allowed (field-goal points below the scorer's norm on the same number of shots), free throws allowed and turnovers forced. Each part is pulled toward zero by its noise; shot-making is the noisiest, so it is pulled the most and a tough make over a good contest barely moves a defender. Shots allowed is left out on purpose: NBA.com credits a shot to whoever is guarding the shooter at that moment, so help defenders absorb shots their teammates gave up. Results repeat at r = 0.46 to 0.68.

3. Team Defense Impact

How much better his team defends with him on the floor, adjusted for the other nine players. It is Hustle Stats' own lineup-adjusted defense model: a ridge regression of every possession's points on all ten players, with score margin and home court as controls (the same possession data as Hustle Plus-Minus). It credits help defense no matter who gets the shot on his line. It uses the same family of technique as published RAPM numbers, but its settings and starting points are our own, so its values won't match them.

Prior-informed. A plain single-season fit starts every player at zero and barely repeats from one season to the next (r about 0.3). Like DARKO and LEBRON, Team Defense Impact starts each season from what we already know: 60% of the player's previous estimate, plus a box-score-style starting point from last season's D-HPM (steals, blocks, charges, rebounding, deflections). We chose those settings by predicting each next season's possessions from 2021-22 to 2025-26. Carrying 100% of the previous estimate forward repeats the most but predicts worse, a reminder that a metric agreeing with itself isn't the same as being accurate. The chosen version repeats at about r = 0.8.

Checked against DARKO. On the same next-season test, using DARKO's start-of-season defense ratings (darko.app, by Kostya Medvedovsky), our version predicted better in 2023-24, 2024-25 and 2025-26 and worse in 2021-22 and 2022-23, when it had only one or two seasons of history to build on. One caution: a few D-HPM weights were learned from all seasons, so our side of that test carries a little hindsight. The Last 3 seasons view averages the 2023-24 to 2025-26 estimates.

What it can't see

  • Contest quality: matchup totals don't record how close the defender was. That is a separate research project with 2025-26 positional tracking.
  • Switches, schemes and roles still shape the numbers after comparing within position.
  • Guarding stars is a coach's vote of confidence, not proof of stopping them, so difficulty is one third of the index, not all of it.
  • Difficulty and Results are compared within position; Team Defense Impact isn't, so bigs can earn rim-protection credit there that guards can't. Across positions the Index still averages out close to even (centers 56, forwards 49 to 52, guards 48 in the Last 3 seasons view).
  • Turnovers count slightly twice: forced turnovers are part of Results, and steals are part of Team Defense Impact's starting point.
  • The live season compares a smaller, early pool of defenders on partial data, so it is labeled a first look.
  • Off-ball work that never ends in a shot or turnover (denials, deterrence, communication) isn't counted.

Contests

The Contests tab on the Defense page uses NBA.com defensive shot tracking. When a player is the closest defender on a shot, the result is recorded along with what those same shooters normally shoot on that kind of shot. The difference is how much his contests lower their FG%.

  • Zones: rim (under 6 feet), mid-range (other 2-pointers) and 3PT.
  • FG% held: expected FG% minus FG% allowed, in percentage points, pulled toward zero by sample size: (expected makes − makes) / (shots + k). k is estimated per zone: about 130 shots at the rim, 240 in the mid-range and about 4,100 on 3-pointers.
  • Points saved: the zone effects times shots times point value, per 100 shots defended (quality) and per season (quality times volume).

Does it repeat? Rim FG% held repeats at r = 0.55 to 0.82 year to year (players with 400+ shots defended in both seasons), and points saved per 100 at 0.55 to 0.80. Mid-range is weaker (0.23 to 0.42), and 3PT FG% allowed barely repeats at all (−0.01 to 0.15), which is why it is pulled so hard toward zero. Contesting 3s matters; the make rate on the ones you contest is mostly luck.

Limits: closest defender isn't always the responsible defender (late closeouts and help rotations count), and bigs who protect the rim face shots that guards never see, so compare within position.

Stats page

The Stats page carries the standard advanced numbers, so readers can check them against NBA.com and Basketball-Reference. Box, on-court and team numbers are built from NBA play-by-play (all minutes, garbage time included unless a column says otherwise). Possession counts come from the play-by-play, so ratings can differ slightly from sites that estimate possessions from box scores.

  • Shooting: eFG% = (FGM + 0.5 × 3PM) / FGA. TS% = PTS / (2 × (FGA + 0.44 × FTA)). +eFG% = eFG% minus the eFG% a league-average shooter would post on the same shots (NBA.com shot tracking).
  • On-court rates: USG%, AST%, ORB%, DRB%, STL% and BLK% use the team and opponent totals from the possessions he was on the floor for. TOV% = TOV / (FGA + 0.44 × FTA + TOV).
  • Stop%: Dean Oliver's formula: the share of the possessions he defends that end in a stop credited to him (steals, blocks, defensive rebounds, plus his share of forced misses and turnovers).
  • Team: ORTG and DRTG are points per 100 possessions. Schedule-adjusted net rating solves every game's net rating (garbage time removed) for team strength plus home court, lightly regularized. Clutch is the NBA definition. Expected eFG% allowed applies league zone make rates to the shots opponents took.
  • Tracking: hustle, rebounding, passing, drives, catch-and-shoot, pull-ups and touches come from NBA.com player tracking; play types (offense, and team defense) from NBA.com / Synergy.
  • Adjusted: the Numbers toggle pulls each rate toward league average by sample size, with the pull for each stat estimated from that season's data (the same method as Shot Profile). Raw is the standard number.

Left out on purpose: raw plus-minus, clutch win-loss records and opponent 3P% are mostly noise or luck over a season. On/off is included but noisy.

Skill vs noise

Every number on the site is an estimate from a limited sample, so two ideas run through all of it.

Shrinkage

Rates are pulled toward a baseline in proportion to how little data stands behind them, with the pull k estimated from how much real talent varies:

estimate = (makes + k × baseline) / (attempts + k)

Small samples get pulled a lot and big samples barely move, so a hot month can't top a leaderboard.

Likely real or could be noise

Each result carries a noise range from its sample size or the model's uncertainty. Likely real means the result is more than twice its noise range away from average. Could be noise means it may still be real, but the sample can't rule out luck yet.

Role peers

A center and a point guard shouldn't be judged against the same average. Each player can be compared with his closest role peers: the most similar players on usage, playmaking, share of assisted makes, minutes and shot locations. Hustle stats are never used to pick peers, so a player can't be compared only with other hustlers.

Listed positions cover players active from 2023-24 on. For about 180 players whose careers ended earlier, position is estimated from their most similar players (within one position 98% of the time).

Limits

  • Shot tracking measures the closest defender's distance, not how well he contests, and has no shot clock. Its splits include garbage time and can't be cut by opponent. Distance is split only at 10 feet, so dunks and floaters share a bucket.
  • Defenses decide which shots are available, so read Alignment as a signal, not a verdict.
  • Transition defense, closeouts and communication don't show up in either data source.
  • Tracking stats exist only as player totals, so they can't be lineup-adjusted.
  • Age, team changes, injuries and role changes all move trends. Check context before calling a trend development.
